Hiring Fraud Is Slipping Past Screening. What Should Screening Owners Do?

November 10, 2026

If you sell background checks, you have probably heard some version of this from a client: "We hired someone, and the person who showed up was not the person we interviewed." That used to sound like a rare story. New research says it is not rare anymore.

In September 2026, identity security company HYPR released its 2026 State of HR Identity Fraud Detection report, based on a survey of 500 US HR leaders in talent acquisition, HR operations and HR technology roles. The numbers are worth a screening professional's attention, and so is what they say about the conversations you will be having with employers.

What the research found

A few findings stand out:

  • 98% of the HR leaders surveyed said they had experienced candidate fraud firsthand. At the same time, 96% said they were confident their organization would detect it.
  • 68% of hiring fraud cases were uncovered by human instinct, meaning someone noticed something felt off, rather than by a system or control.
  • In 42% of cases, the fraud was found only after employment began. When that happened, it usually took four to six days to surface, and the report says 98% of those fraudulent hires already had company credentials by then.
  • Screening was the most common place fraud was caught (52%), but interviews, onboarding, technical assessments and active employment were all close behind. No single checkpoint stopped it reliably.
  • About a quarter of organizations (24%) spent one to three months resolving a single fake hire.

HYPR's report also draws on a separate HYPR study of IT security leaders, and it labels which figures come from which survey. HYPR sells identity security products, so read its conclusions with that in mind. The survey data is still a useful signal about what HR teams are dealing with.

Why this matters to screening businesses

The report's core point is simple: identity checks tend to happen at one moment, and fraud finds the gaps between moments. Employers are worried, they are not sure who owns the problem, and many only spend money on it after something goes wrong.

For a screening business, that creates a real opening. Employers do not need another vendor who just sends a report. They need someone who will pick up the phone, explain what each part of a screening package does and does not cover, and help them think about where identity questions fit in their own hiring process.

That is a sales and service conversation, not a software feature. And it is exactly the kind of conversation experienced screening salespeople are good at.

Questions employers are starting to ask

If you are talking to HR teams this quarter, expect questions like these:

  1. How do we know the person we screened is the person who starts on day one?
  2. What does our current screening package actually confirm, and what does it not?
  3. Who on our side should own identity questions between the offer and the first day?
  4. What should we do when a check comes back and something does not match?

You do not need to have a product for every one of these. You do need to answer honestly, point clients to the right checks, and tell them when a question belongs with their own HR, IT or legal team. Candid answers build trust faster than a long feature list.
