The EEOC Dropped Its Criminal Records Guidance. Your Clients Will Ask What Changed.

November 24, 2026

When a federal agency changes how it talks about criminal background checks, employers notice. Some of them will call the person who sells them screening and ask a simple question: does this change what we do?

That is exactly what happened this fall. If you sell background checks, or you are thinking about owning a screening business, this is a good example of why the relationship with the client matters as much as the report.

What happened

On October 28, 2026, the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission voted to rescind its 2012 Enforcement Guidance on the Consideration of Arrest and Conviction Records in Employment Decisions Under Title VII. The agency announced it in a press release on October 29. The rescission took effect immediately.

The 2012 guidance was the document many employers and screening companies pointed to when they talked about looking at criminal records role by role instead of using blanket exclusions.

What the EEOC said did not change

The press release is short, and it is worth reading in full. A few points stand out:

  • Older policy statements are not coming back. The EEOC said the rescission does not restore the policy statements that the 2012 guidance replaced.
  • Employers do not have to undo lawful practices. The agency said employers are not required to abandon lawful practices they adopted in response to the guidance.
  • Discrimination is still prohibited. EEOC Chair Andrea Lucas said the move "does not give employers license to engage in unlawful discrimination," and that Title VII still prohibits discrimination based on race or national origin.

Employment law firms have also pointed out that state and local fair chance and ban the box laws are not affected by a federal guidance change, and that the Fair Credit Reporting Act still applies when employers use third-party background reports. DLA Piper and Fisher Phillips both published summaries for employers.

This post is not legal advice. Employers should talk to their own counsel about their hiring policies.

Why this matters if you sell screening

Headlines like this create confusion. Some employers will read "EEOC drops criminal records guidance" and assume the rules got simpler. Others will worry they now have to rewrite everything. Neither reaction is a good place to make hiring decisions from.

The screening provider who picks up the phone, explains what the agency actually said, and points the client back to their own counsel for policy decisions earns trust. The one who sends a link and disappears does not.

Clients rarely leave a screening provider because of the software. They leave when nobody answers, when questions sit in an inbox, or when reports come back with no one to explain them.

What this means if you want to own

If you are a screening sales rep thinking about running your own company, moments like this are a fair test. Ask yourself:

  1. Who answers when a client calls with a question about a report or a rule change?
  2. Who handles the email that follows?
  3. Who does the actual background check processing while you are out selling?
  4. Who keeps you sharp on how to talk about changes like this without overstepping?

If the answer to all four is "me, alone," owning gets hard fast. Software by itself does not answer the phone.
